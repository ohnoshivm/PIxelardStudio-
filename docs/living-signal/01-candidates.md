# 01 — Candidate Executions of THE LIVING SIGNAL (pick list)

> **Six candidates for the studio to choose from.**
> - **A–D** came from the multi-agent divergence run (typographic and material lenses), each with its own harsh self-critique. The run was stopped early at the studio's request, so the generative, optics and choreographic lenses never ran.
> - **E–F** were written by the creative lead to cover those missing angles: light and time, and generative/evolutionary.
>
> All six share the core concept: a **Living Identity** (a system, not a static logo) plus a **Distribution Machine** (one signal becoming every format and channel). **None of them fixes a logo, palette, typeface or material.** Those are chosen only after a direction is approved.
>
> Placeholders throughout: no real clients, results or awards are claimed.

## The creative lead's honest read
1. **A, Every Piece Is Whole.** Best positioning for a *branding-first* studio whose edge is distribution. The identity is a typographic rule, so it works everywhere, including static print, search and AI answers. It is light to build and has the highest long-term brand potential. Weakness: it is the least "wow 3D", and its lineage (Whitney W) must be pushed past.
2. **B, Cane.** The most original physical idea and the most beautiful single image. The favicon cut is a genuinely new signature moment. It is the hardest look to get right in WebGL.
3. **C, Family Mould.** The clearest *distribution* metaphor (one shot, every channel, at once), with an unforgettable one-beat moment. The toy and plastic connotations need careful art direction.
4. **F, Selection.** The strongest *AI-first* story and the only one that dramatises testing and iteration (performance marketing). The base mark must be excellent.
5. **E, Exposure.** The strongest for motion and video capabilities; recognisable from a single still. The cliché risk is real.
6. **D, In Any Key.** Poetic, but it needs sound to land. The weakest fit.

**How to choose.** Pick **one** to develop to final, or **up to three** for keyframe comparison. You can also say "A, but with B's favicon cut". Ideas graft well between these:
- A's back mark, B's favicon cut and C's one-beat fill are all portable.

After you choose, I render real homepage keyframes (three.js + typography), iterate on them, and then wait for approval before fixing the identity.

## A — Every Piece Is Whole

> A single typeset sheet that you cut with the cursor. Every piece instantly re-sets itself into a complete composition in a real channel format, so the visitor performs the Distribution Machine and watches the identity survive every cut.

**Core idea.** The press sheet and the cut. In print, a job is set once on a big sheet, then imposed and cut into everything it needs to become. Pixelards treats a brand the same way. The Living Identity is the sheet: a set of setting rules rather than a fixed artwork. The Distribution Machine is the cut, which turns the sheet into stories, posts, billboards, emails, search listings and product pages. The difference from print is that nothing is ever cropped: every piece re-sets its own type so that it is complete in itself. The house proportion makes the promise literal. The master sheet is 1:√2, the only rectangle whose halves have the same shape as the whole (the basis of DIN 476 / ISO 216 'A' paper), so a half-cut produces two whole sheets. Reconnection is literal too. Gathered pieces carry stepped back marks that line up into one diagonal, the bookbinder's proof that a set belongs together.

**Genome: what never changes**
- One voice: a single variable type family (working name 'Pixelards Setting') with width, weight and optical-size axes. Brand type is always set in it, in sentence case, never in letter-spaced caps.
- The fit rule: a piece's primary line always fills its measure exactly. The width and optical-size axes do the fitting; type is never scaled as an image. The wordmark is therefore a rule, not a drawing: 'Pixelards, set to fill'.
- One permitted break: when the name has to stack, it breaks only as 'Pixel / ards', so the plural ending (one studio, many outputs) always gets a line of its own.
- No crops: pieces are always re-set, never cropped. Type never touches a cut edge, and margins are always 1/12 of the piece's short side.
- The back mark: a solid bar in the proof colour (1:5, never a square) at the spine edge of every piece. Its position steps down by sequence number, so a gathered set forms one continuous diagonal.
- House proportion 1:√2 for every owned surface: decks, posters, site cards and the hero sheet.
- A fixed hierarchy ladder: claim, then name, then stacked name, then '-ards', then the back mark. As a piece shrinks it steps down one rung at a time and never skips a rung.
- Two inks on any stock: one dark ink, plus one proof colour reserved for the back mark and the active cut.

**Genome: what adapts**
- Format and aspect ratio, from a 16 px favicon to a roadside billboard: 9:16, 4:5, 1:1, 1.91:1, 2:3 street poster, ~4:1 roadside, 600 px email, and formats measured in characters
- Physical size and viewing distance, which set the optical-size axis and how far down the hierarchy ladder a piece sits
- Width and weight axis values, solved per piece by the fit engine
- Copy and language: line breaks, line lengths, and which pre-approved variant of the claim fits each measure
- Stock (paper colour or screen theme) per channel or client
- Light angle and colour temperature in rendered and motion pieces, following the time of day in Sydney
- Sequence position within a set, which sets the back-mark offset
- The cut plan (how a campaign is divided), set by the brief or by the visitor

**The signal unit.** The fitted line with its back mark. This is a behaviour, not a finished logo: the word 'Pixelards' set to fill exactly whatever measure it is given, with a short proof-coloured bar at the spine. TRANSFORMS: across a 4:1 strip it runs at maximum width (wdth ~150) and light weight. In a 9:16 story it stacks as 'Pixel / ards', narrow and heavier. At favicon size it becomes '-ards', and below that only the bar remains. MULTIPLIES: every cut produces two complete marks, not two halves of one. ADAPTS: optical size follows physical size and viewing distance, so a billboard setting has tight counters and a 12 px setting has opened apertures. TRAVELS: each piece leaves for its channel carrying its back mark like a stamp of origin. RECONNECTS: gather any set and the back marks snap into one diagonal. At the end of the site, 'Pixel' and 'ards', which the visitor's first cut split across two pieces, rejoin on one line.

**Art direction.** Editorial, tactile, daylight. The page is a deep graphite 'composing stone': matte and warm, never pure black. The world is a sheet of heavy uncoated board lying on it, with square corners and a visible 1 mm edge. A single low raking key light (12-25° elevation) follows the real sun position in Sydney: steep and neutral at midday, long and amber late in the day. There is no fill light, only light bounced off the paper. COMPOSITION: an asymmetric 12-column Swiss grid. The plain sentence sits in columns 1-5 at reading size, and the sheet occupies columns 6-12 as the stage. THE KEYFRAME: after a few cuts, a contact sheet of perfectly set rectangles, each a complete composition, lying under one light with long soft shadows. PALETTE DIRECTIONS (not final): (1) 'Proof': unbleached stock, carbon ink and a fluorescent proof pink for the back mark and the cut. (2) 'Board': grey-brown recycled board, soot black and safety orange. (3) 'Night run': graphite stock, bone ink and sodium yellow. EXPLICITLY AVOIDS: crop marks, registration targets and CMYK swatches used as decoration (the print-nerd cliché); green self-healing cutting mats; letterpress and kraft-paper nostalgia; Mondrian-style primary-colour partitions; glossy phone and billboard mockups; rounded cards on an off-white page; blue or green accents; and any pixel or grid-square motif.

**Typography.** DIRECTION 1, 'Wide-range grotesk'. The fit must come from the drawing, not from scaling, so the family needs an extreme width axis. Open source for prototyping: Anybody (Etcetera Type Co, wdth 50-150, wght 100-900) and Roboto Flex (opsz 8-144, wdth 25-151, plus a grade axis). Licensed: GT Flexa (Grilli Type) or Obviously (OH no Type Co). DIRECTION 2, 'Optical-size newspaper serif'. The identity re-sets the way a news story moves from a front-page headline to a 7 pt caption. Open source: Newsreader (Production Type, opsz 6-72). Licensed: Tiempos Headline / Text / Fine (Klim) or GT Super Display / Text, used as optical cuts. In this direction the fit is solved by tracking (±2% max), optical size and sense-based line breaks rather than by width. LONG TERM: a custom 'Pixelards Setting' family with wdth 50-170, opsz and wght, plus an '-ards' ligature in which d and s share one knife-cut terminal. COMPOSITION: one size per piece (no size soup). Labels use the same family at a small optical size with tabular figures, never a mono. Captions name the format in plain words, e.g. 'Story, 1080 × 1920'. HOW THE TYPE LIVES: on reflow, words physically travel to their new lines (FLIP, in reading order) while the axes interpolate; nothing fades. Links tighten by 6% width on hover, so even the UI behaves as typesetting. Line breaks follow phrase boundaries pre-marked in the copy, never character counts alone.

**3D forms and materials.** THE SHEET: rigid board of about 600 gsm, modelled as a thin box (thickness 0.5% of its short side). It carries a procedural fibre normal map (a low-frequency cloud plus high-frequency streaks along the grain) at roughness ~0.85. INK: a flat matte layer, slightly darker than the stock's shadow tone, with a 1-2% dot-gain halo at glyph edges so type reads as printed rather than overlaid. CUT EDGES have their own material: brighter, fibrous and slightly translucent, so a lifted piece throws a hairline glint along its edge in the raking light (the 'cut glint'). SHADOWS: separated pieces cast analytic soft rectangle shadows onto the graphite ground, lengthening as they lift 1-2%. THE CUT is not an object (no blade, no scissors). It is a 1-device-pixel line that flashes to the proof colour for one frame, then opens into a gap equal to the margin unit. WHY NOT STOCK: the only object is a rectangle of board, the most ordinary thing in print, made precious by real light, real thickness and the behaviour of the type on it. There are no glossy forms, chrome, glass or particles. The hero object is the format itself.

**Motion.** Set, cut, settle. (1) Cuts are instant: a one-frame proof-colour flash (16 ms), then the gap opens over ~280 ms on a critically damped spring (SecondOrder f 2.6 Hz, ζ 1.0, r 0). Paper does not bounce. (2) Type never fades. Words travel to their new lines over 360 ms (cubic-out) with a 12 ms stagger in reading order while the axes interpolate. The whole cut-to-rest takes ≤ 600 ms. (3) Mass follows area: spring frequency scales with 1/√area, so big pieces move slower than small ones. (4) Section transitions are guillotine wipes: the page parts along one straight line at the section boundary, scroll-scrubbed and exactly reversible. (5) The Gather is the inverse of the cut, but brisker: pieces stack at 240 ms each with 60% overlap, and the back marks lock with a final 80 ms alignment. (6) The idle state is stillness. Only the light moves, at the real speed of the sun, so the page reads as calm and confident rather than wobbly. Character: a sub-editor's red pen, decisive action followed by a calm settle.

**Hero and interaction.** HERO TOY: CUT THE SHEET. H1 (3 s): as the pointer moves over the sheet, a hairline cut guide appears across it, perpendicular to its long side. The type in both would-be pieces ghost-previews its re-set at 20% ink, so the visitor sees the claim survive before committing. The plain sentence sits beside the sheet. H2: cutting is distribution, and each piece re-setting itself is the living identity, so the toy is the thesis. H3: the DOM sentence sits in columns 1-5 and the sheet in columns 6-12. Later the sheet's edges grow to become the page's edges. H4: the preloader's widening measure becomes the sheet's top line. H5: every click is a cut, producing new formats and new compositions. Cutting deeper walks down the hierarchy ladder until the smallest pieces carry only '-ards' or the back mark, so the visitor discovers the floor of the system. One pink detent sits at the exact half: cut there and you get two 1:√2 sheets with identical compositions at 0.707 scale ('Half. Still whole.'), endlessly repeatable. Double-click or G gathers the pieces: the back marks align and the sheet re-forms (reconnect). H6: the cut plan (a list of rectangles) is kept in memory, with sessionStorage as a convenience only; the site renders correctly without it. It returns as the layout of the Work index and as the visitor's proof sheet at Contact. DETENTS: the guide snaps, within 3%, to cuts that produce real channel ratios (9:16, 4:5, 1:1, 1.91:1, 2:3, ~4:1) and labels the outcome ('1:1 post | 9:16 story'). CURSOR elsewhere stays the system cursor; there is no custom-cursor gimmick. KEYBOARD: the stage is focusable. Arrow keys move the guide between detents, Enter cuts, Backspace undoes and G gathers. A live region announces 'Cut. Two pieces: square post and story.' TOUCH: a tap cuts at the tap point along the piece's short axis, and press-and-hold previews. There are no swipe gestures, so scrolling is never captured. REDUCED MOTION: cuts and re-sets are instant, with only a 120 ms opacity change. SOUND (opt-in): one dry board-cut sound, and a softer stack sound on gather.

**Generative system.** INPUTS: (a) a signal document: the claim (≤ 9 words, phrase boundaries marked), a short line (≤ 4 words), the name, the micro form ('-ards'), and approved claim variants for character budgets; (b) a stock; (c) a cut plan or a list of target formats (pixel or physical dimensions plus viewing distance); (d) sequence order; (e) locale. RULES: (1) compute the minimum legible cap height from the viewing distance. (2) Choose the highest hierarchy rung that fits at or above that size with wdth between 60 and 160. (3) Break lines at the marked phrase boundaries, then balance them. (4) Solve wdth so the longest line fills the measure; take opsz from physical size and wght from the rung. (5) Set margins to the short side ÷ 12. (6) Place the back mark at the spine, offset by sequence position ÷ set size. (7) Where a channel strips the typeface (search, AI answers, notifications, alt text), count the measure in characters and choose the claim variant that fits. OUTPUTS: PNG and MP4 for social (9:16, 4:5, 1:1); PDF for print (posters, roadside); HTML email headers with live text; title and description strings; product-page heroes built with container queries; and a 6-second spot in which a sheet is cut on camera into the campaign set. A client receives their own sheet, rules and engine. A campaign becomes one signal plus one cut plan, so a 300-size rollout is a render, not a week of resizing. The family resemblance comes from the invariants; the variety comes from the solver.

**Distribution, made visible.** 'THE IMPOSITION', the homepage's immersive set piece. The camera looks straight down at a vast 1:√2 press sheet with the studio's signal set across it once. As the visitor scrolls (scrubbed, reversible), real cut lines draw on as hairlines and the sheet is cut in sequence. Each piece slides out, turns to its real-world orientation and re-sets itself, captioned only with its format and spec: 9:16 story, 4:5 feed post, 1:1 post, 1.91:1 link card, 2:3 street poster, ~4:1 roadside billboard, a 600 px email header (live text), a product-page hero (a real responsive DOM section), and a 6-second spot (the piece plays a loop of itself being cut). Two more pieces carry no brand type at all: a plain-text search listing (title fitted to about 60 characters, description to about 155) and the one-sentence answer an AI assistant might quote. Their caption reads: 'Here the typeface is stripped away. What survives is the sentence, so we write it to fit.' Then comes THE GATHER: every piece stacks spine-on, and the back marks lock into one unbroken pink diagonal, visible proof of continuity. Reverse scroll fans the pieces back out. There are no reach numbers, arcs, globes or dashboards, only formats, specs and type. Content is the studio's own signal, or client placeholders flagged placeholder: true.

**Signature moment.** THE GATHER. At the climax of the Imposition, about ten pieces fly in and stack on edge within ~900 ms: story, posts, poster, roadside, email, search listing, answer sentence, product page and spot. Each lands one step lower than the last, and their back marks lock into a single unbroken proof-pink diagonal, with one dry paper sound if sound is on. In one second the visitor sees that a wildly varied campaign is one thing. Reverse scroll fans it back out. Its quiet twin lives in the hero: the pink half-cut detent, where the sheet splits into two smaller copies of itself with identical compositions ('Half. Still whole.').

**References (lineage, not things to copy).** Experimental Jetset: Whitney Museum of American Art identity, the 'Responsive W' (2013) (The key precedent for a mark defined as a rule that fills any format. We move the principle from one drawn zigzag to a typeset word on variable axes, and add the cut, the hierarchy ladder and the gather, which the W does not have.); Karl Gerstner: Designing Programmes (1964) (Design as a programme rather than a solution. The fit engine is a Gerstner programme run in real time on a visitor's input.); Josef Müller-Brockmann: Grid Systems in Graphic Design (1981) (Proportional margins and grids as invariants. Here the grid is solved per piece rather than drawn once.); DIN 476 / ISO 216 paper sizes (Walter Porstmann; DIN standard 1922) (The 1:√2 ratio whose halves keep the shape of the whole is the literal, verifiable basis of 'every piece is whole'. Any earlier attribution, such as Lichtenberg's 1786 letter, must be verified before it appears in copy.); Bookbinding collating marks (back marks / stepped collation) (A real production device. Stepped marks on folded signatures form a diagonal when gathered in the right order. We use it as the identity's reconnect invariant, not as print decoration.); David Jonathan Ross: Fit (typeface) (A display face built to fill extreme widths. It is lineage for the fit rule, and also proof that fit alone isn't an identity, which is why we add the break, the ladder and the back mark.); Ethan Marcotte: 'Responsive Web Design', A List Apart (2010) (Content that adapts to its container. We turn a web technique into the brand's personality and extend it to print, OOH and type-less channels.); Gordon Matta-Clark: Splitting (1974) (The cut as an act that reveals rather than destroys. We take the tone, not the gesture: our cut is straight, calm and constructive.); Robert Bringhurst: The Elements of Typographic Style (1992) (Measure, hierarchy and setting as ethics. The site's craft claim lives or dies on line breaks and measure.)

**Self-critique.** Nearest precedents: Experimental Jetset's Whitney 'Responsive W' is the obvious ancestor, and 'variable font fills the width' (DJR's Fit, countless CodePens and type-foundry specimen sites) has made fit-to-measure type a familiar trick. Recursive rectangle subdivision is a generative-art staple that slides easily into Mondrian pastiche, and a cut-the-paper toy invites comparisons with slicing games. Paper under raking light on a dark ground is a well-worn 'premium print studio' look. The back-mark diagonal is an insider print reference that most visitors will read as a progress bar or a stray decoration. Strategically, the idea is strongest on formats and weakest on distribution over time: search, automation and performance get reduced to 'more rectangles'. The type-less search and answer pieces are honest but visually anticlimactic, and the whole thing could position Pixelards as a beautiful layout studio rather than a growth partner. The toy is destructive: if 'every piece is whole' doesn't land at once, cutting reads as shredding the brand. Fitted type at extreme ratios gets ugly (ultra-condensed towers in slivers), so detents have to refuse bad cuts, which can feel restrictive and 'designed for you'. Live reflow stands or falls on line-break quality; one clumsy break kills the thesis on the hero. The wordmark is never the same drawing twice, so trademark protection and recognisability at small sizes depend on a custom typeface that will take 6-12 months and real budget. The interim open-source faces (Anybody, Roboto Flex) are generic and won't carry recognition on their own. On mobile the sheet is small, and a few taps produce unusable pieces. The √2 'half-cut' fact is lovely, but every designer knows it, so it delights insiders more than clients. The hero's pointer-previews / click-varies structure follows the reference's H1/H5 rhythm closely, even though the content differs.

**Feasibility.** Unusually DOM-first. Every piece is a real DOM element with live text: CSS variable fonts via font-variation-settings, container queries, and text-wrap: balance as a baseline. The toy therefore works with zero WebGL, stays accessible and selectable, and WebGL only adds material. FIT ENGINE: harfbuzzjs (HarfBuzz compiled to WebAssembly, ~250 KB, lazy-loaded in a Web Worker) computes glyph advances for any axis values without layout thrash. A binary search on wdth (≤ 8 iterations per line) runs only on cut and resize, never per frame. Ghost previews are precomputed in idle time for each piece's magnetic detents (≤ 6 per piece), so hovering never solves anything live. WEBGL: one persistent R3F canvas renders the board slabs (instanced boxes), the fibre normals, a raking directional light, analytic soft rectangle shadows (closed form, no shadow maps) and the cut glint, all bound to DOM rects. Because the pieces are rigid board, DOM text rides them via CSS matrix3d computed from the same camera (the CSS3DRenderer approach). Scroll timelines use GSAP ScrollTrigger as pure functions of progress; springs use SecondOrder. BUDGETS: ≤ 16 live pieces and ≤ 30 draw calls. Fonts are subset to Latin WOFF2 (≤ 120 KB) with font-display: swap and a metric-matched fallback. 3D lazy-loads after text is interactive. RISKS: animating font-variation-settings forces text layout every frame. The mitigation is to move words with FLIP transforms, interpolate axes on at most two pieces at once and snap the rest. CSS 3D can drift a pixel from WebGL on high-DPR mobile; on low tiers we drop the WebGL and use DOM cards with CSS shadows. In-browser export of the visitor's set renders the same layouts to canvas or SVG and is deterministic. MOBILE: 30 fps is easy. The main risk is sheet size, so on mobile the sheet fills the width and cut depth is capped at 8 pieces. Reduced motion, keyboard use, 200% zoom and WebGL failure keep all content.

**How AI-first shows.** The site acts out the operating method: the visitor decides where to cut, and the system sets the type, instantly, every time, by the same rules. That is AI-first as behaviour, with rules generating families of outputs and no artwork redrawn. In the studio, a brand ships as a signal document plus a setting engine. Language models draft claim variants for specific measures (a 60-character search title, a three-word square, a twelve-word roadside), the fit engine accepts or rejects each one by rule, and people approve. Every sheet is versioned. The hero's colophon shows the studio's own claim history (v1.0 → v1.3, with earlier versions struck through like editorial revisions), so learning reads as revision, not as a chart. A/B testing is shown as 'two cuts of one sheet', never as invented uplift. The Imposition shows speed of iteration by cutting a whole campaign in one scroll. There are no brains, sparkles, robots or gradients anywhere; the intelligence is visible only as type that always lands well.

## B — CANE: Pulled, not redrawn

> The identity is built like Venetian glass cane: one designed cross-section runs the whole length, so the brand can be pulled to any size, pressed to fit any frame and cut into as many pieces as the channels need, without ever being redrawn.

**Core idea.** Glass cane (murrine). A cane is a glass rod whose cross-section is a designed pattern. Pulled while hot, it gets longer and thinner and the pattern stays the same, only smaller. Pressed on the marver, its outline takes a new proportion and the inner order stays put. Sliced, every slice carries the whole pattern. Bundle several canes, fuse them and pull again, and you get a new cane that contains the old ones. That gives the Living Signal in one material. The SECTION is the Living Identity: its order never changes, while its proportion, size, colour, twist and temperature do. PULL / PRESS / CUT / BUNDLE is the Distribution Machine: scale to the channel, fit the format, multiply into units, recombine into campaigns. The website is one continuous cane. The hero looks at its end, the rest of the site runs along its length as the spine, and the footer keeps pulling into the next page.

**Genome: what never changes**
- The mark is always a cut face seen end-on, never an outline or a drawing. If you cannot see that it was cut from something longer, it is not the mark.
- Fixed topology: clear casing, then an outer colour layer, then an inner colour layer, then the core, plus one filament set in the thick side. Pressing changes proportions; the order and the count never change.
- The eccentric lean: every layer is offset toward the same upper-forward quarter, so the section reads as moving and never as a target, eye, vinyl record or cable.
- The filament is always present, down to 16 px, and is always the hottest colour in any palette. It is the single signal colour.
- Gloss break: the cut face is ground and polished against a softer, fire-polished body. That contrast says the piece was cut from a longer rod.

**Genome: what adapts**
- PRESS (outer proportion): the casing takes the format's aspect (9:16, 4:5, 1:1, 16:9, 1.91:1, a wide billboard band). Inner layers flow with it and keep their order.
- PULL (scale): the diameter follows channel size, from a building wrap down to a 16 px favicon. Below fixed pixel thresholds the layers merge in a fixed order, so small sizes are hinted rather than shrunk.
- Layer colours per client, campaign, audience or locale; the filament stays the hottest hue.
- Filament angle (twist): the filament rotates along the cane's length, so a slice's filament angle is a timestamp or version number (one full turn per year, or per campaign).
- Glass type per medium: clear and light-transmitting for screens, opal or opaque for print, end-on cast-resin sections for signage.
- Temperature as state: glowing means live or in production; cooled means shipped. Colour only appears once the glass is cool.
- Length and twist rate set duration and energy in motion pieces.
- Bundle size: 1, 7 or 19 sub-canes, hex-packed, for campaigns, capability sets or co-branding.

**The signal unit.** THE SECTION. This describes behaviour, not a final logo. TRANSFORMS: pressed between formats, the casing changes aspect and the layers flow with it like soft glass. The press is a superellipse (Lp-norm) metric that moves from round to near-rectangular, never a raster stretch. MULTIPLIES: every cut yields a slice (a murrina) that is a complete mark, and one cane yields hundreds. ADAPTS: pulled thin, it keeps its pattern. Below 48 px the inner layer merges. At 24 px it reads as casing, eccentric core and filament. At 16 px it is casing plus filament dot and still reads as the lean. TRAVELS: the cane is the site's spine. Slices become the section index marks, the cursor's rest state and, at the end, the browser favicon. RECONNECTS: slices and canes are hex-packed, fused and re-cased into larger canes. A campaign, the capability set, or a client-plus-Pixelards collaboration is literally one section made of sections, and the bundle's own cut face becomes a new mark that visibly contains the originals.

**Art direction.** Look and feel: a glass hot shop seen through an editorial lens; slow, warm and exact. Camera grammar is macro photography: 50–90 mm equivalent, shallow depth of field, rack focus between the polished cut face and the body. Composition: big type in the left five columns. At right, the cane is end-on, its face about 40vh across, its body receding out of frame to the right, so the source is always off-page. After the hero, the cane turns side-on and becomes a thin horizontal spine running through the site, with no card and no rounded window. Light: one hard, high key light with the quality of a skylight or high southern sun, laying a single long specular line down the cylinder. The hot glass is the only warm second source. No rim-light theatrics. Detail: stretched air-lines inside the glass show the pull direction, and a few conchoidal micro-chips sit at the cut rim. Palette directions (not final): (1) LEHR: a warm soot near-black ground; the incandescent ramp is reserved for heat; cooled layers in dense opaque murrine colours (oxblood, opal white, ochre, deep ink); filament in the vermilion-to-cadmium range. (2) NOON: a sun-bleached mineral pale ground, clear glass throwing hard caustics onto the page, ink-black type, a single vermilion filament. (3) OPALINE: milk-glass white bodies, carbon-black cores, the filament as the only colour. It explicitly avoids: dichroic rainbow glass, iridescent 'AI' gradients, frosted glassmorphism panels, chrome, bokeh dust, molten-metal stock looks, pixel clusters, and any off-white page with a dark rounded card.

**Typography.** Direction 1, CUT SERIF. Display: GT Sectra (Grilli Type, licensed). It is a serif built from broad-nib logic and scalpel cuts, and both the name and the cuts suit a 'section'. Open-source alternative: Fraunces (variable opsz/wght/SOFT/WONK). Labels: Söhne Mono (Klim, licensed) or DM Mono (open), 11–12 px uppercase, used as cut coordinates (CUT 04 · 1,240 MM). Type behaviour: headlines arrive 'hot' and set as they cool. With Fraunces, the SOFT axis eases from 100 to 0 while the colour moves from incandescent to ink over about 700 ms, so the type literally sets. Direction 2, PRESSED GROTESK. Display: GT Flexa (Grilli, licensed, variable width) or Mona Sans (open, wdth 75–125). Type behaviour: a headline's width axis is set by the aspect of the frame it sits in: compressed inside a 9:16 slice, extended across the billboard band. The same words are pressed along with the section. Editorial composition for both: one generous column and one statement per screen. Section numbers are replaced by distance along the cane, so the page is measured in millimetres of glass ('You are 340 mm into Pixelards'). Captions sit under slices like museum labels. Type is never set over the 3D glass. A cut face hosts type only inside format mockups, where it is the ground.

**3D forms and materials.** THE CANE: a long glass rod. It has a clear soda-lime casing (with the faint green cast real casing shows at a cut edge), two opaque coloured layers and an opaque core in an eccentric nest, and one solid filament rod. The body is fire-polished with a single long highlight. Cut faces are ground and polished with crisp speculars. Inside are stretched air-lines, and the rim has micro-chips. THE NECK: when pulled, the rod forms a smooth hourglass taper in which the pattern visibly miniaturises (the money shot). THE SLICES: thin discs whose clear casing ring carries light as a bright rim. Pressed slices become rectangles with slumped, glass-softened corners, not UI corner radii. HEAT: a blackbody emission ramp hides the layer colours while the glass is hot and reveals them as it cools, as real coloured glass does. Why this is not stock: every surface event is a process truth (pull, press, cut, cool, bundle) that carries meaning. The form exists to answer one question: how does a mark survive every size and format? No glossy object ever floats free. The cane is always attached to a source off-frame or visibly cut from one.

**Motion.** Viscous and thermal, then decisive. (1) Things are drawn, not tweened. Transitions follow a 'draw' curve: a viscous slow start, an accelerating middle and a settling neck. (2) Heat before colour. Anything new arrives glowing and monochrome, and its colour blooms as it cools (decay tau about 0.7 s, full colour in 0.6–1.2 s). (3) Long things sag. The side-on spine is a 48-node spring chain with slight gravity, so scroll velocity bends it a little. (4) Cuts are the only fast moves: a 50 ms snap and a clean tick, then the slice spins and settles in two visible wobbles (SecondOrder about 4 Hz, damping about 0.45). (5) Input-follow is heavy and near-critically damped (about 1.1 Hz, damping about 0.95), because glass does not flick. Signature transitions: PRESS (a frame changes aspect over 380–520 ms; its sides bow out up to 3% mid-move and settle flat with sharp corners), TURN (the end-on circle becomes the side-on line, handing off from hero to site), and DRAW (scroll travels along the cane as a pure function of progress, exact in reverse). Reduced motion: cuts and presses become instant state swaps, heat is a static warm tint, and the spine does not sag.

**Hero and interaction.** Hero toy: PULL, CUT, PRESS. MOVE: the cane's end turns toward the cursor on a heavy spring. In three-quarter view the layered interior shows through the clear casing, and glass near the pointer warms with a soft emissive bloom. DRAG (horizontal, on the stage only): you pull. The cane lengthens and a glowing neck forms, while the cut face shrinks with perfect fidelity. A small neck 'handle' nudges after 2 s idle as the affordance. CLICK/TAP: cut. A slice snaps off and drops into a tray under the headline, pressed to the next format in sequence (1:1, 9:16, 16:9, billboard band, favicon) and labelled. The cane was gathered with colour that changes along its length, so every cut is a new colourway, and the filament has rotated further (time). H1: within 3 s you see glass craft, the end answers your pointer, and one plain sentence sits in its own column. H2: the toy demonstrates the thesis: scale without redrawing (pull), multiplication (cut) and adaptation (press). H3: type keeps its own column; the stage frame is the cane's casing outline, which becomes the transition device when the circle becomes a line at TURN. H4: the preloader gathers the cane, so the loading object is the hero object. H5: every click yields a new slice, a new format and a new colourway, and five slices read as a family. H6: your slices persist (session state, with optional localStorage). They become the site's index marks, set the colourway of the favicon cut at the end of THE PULL, and become sub-canes in the final bundle at Contact. Elsewhere on the site, the cursor rests as a tiny slice in your latest colourway. It presses to a narrow bar over text links and to a square over media. Scroll travels along the cane. Keyboard parity: arrow keys pull and Enter cuts. On touch, touch-action: pan-y means vertical scroll is never hijacked, and the pull is horizontal and stage-only, kept clear of the screen edges to avoid the iOS back-swipe. Sound (opt-in): low furnace air, a ring that rises in pitch as the rod thins, and a clean tink per cut.

**Generative system.** A cane is a recipe, not a drawing. INPUTS: topology (layer list, thicknesses, eccentricity vector, filament position), palette (layer colours, filament hue), format (aspect, safe area), target size (px or mm), medium (emissive, print, motion), time (filament angle) and bundle spec (which sub-canes, packing). RULES: preserve order. Press with a superellipse metric so curvature tightens predictably. Merge layers at size thresholds, keep the filament at 1.5 px or 0.4 mm minimum, and check colour contrast per medium. Bundles pack hexagonally (1, 7, 19) and are re-cased. OUTPUTS: SVG and PDF sections for print, icons and favicons; GLSL-rendered slices and 'flythrough' films (travelling down the cane as the filament rotates) for motion and digital OOH; format layouts where the pressed section is the ground for copy and imagery; and a 'cane card' spec per brand. FAMILIES: one recipe yields a year of social tiles (365 slices, each dated by filament angle), campaign bundles, physical merch made as true cross-sections (actual glass or cast-resin slices, coasters, pins) and end-on signage. For clients, Pixelards builds each brand its own cane recipe, so the studio's service is visible in its own mark.

**Distribution, made visible.** THE PULL is a full-bleed, scroll-scrubbed set piece. The cane is drawn left to right across the page and gets thinner at each station. At nine stations it is pressed and cut. Each cut face turns to camera and hands off from WebGL to DOM as a format in true proportion, for a clearly labelled placeholder brand: a 9:16 story, a 4:5 feed post, a 1:1 post, a 16:9 film still, a 1.91:1 link card, a wide billboard band, a 600 px email header, a search-result entry (with the slice as its favicon and an honest placeholder title and description), and a product tile. Each carries its spec in mono: dimensions, safe area, and what varied (press, size, colour, filament angle). There are no reach, click or result numbers. Finally the hair-thin end is cut, and its 16 px face replaces the browser-tab favicon in the visitor's own colourway from the hero. The signal visibly leaves the page. Scrolling back reverses everything exactly. On phones the cane runs down the page instead of across, and the formats stack.

**Signature moment.** The favicon cut. At the end of THE PULL, nine formats have resolved on the page. The cane is drawn hair-thin, there is one tick, and the last 16 px slice, in the colourway the visitor cut in the hero, drops out of the page and appears in the browser tab as the site's favicon. The identity has been pulled continuously on screen from billboard thickness to favicon size without being redrawn, and it now lives in the visitor's own browser chrome. Where dynamic favicons are unsupported, it lands in a DOM tab-strip mock instead.

**References (lineage, not things to copy).** Venetian murrine and millefiori (Murano glass cane tradition) (The process truth the concept rests on: a patterned cross-section that survives pulling, pressing and slicing. We take the logic, not the flower patterns or the bead aesthetic.); Hellenistic and Roman mosaic glass bowls (cane slices fused into vessels) (Early evidence that many slices from canes can be recombined into a new whole. This is the lineage of our RECONNECT (bundle) step.); Lino Tagliapietra (Murano glass maestro; reticello, incalmo, cane work) (The quality bar for cane craft that the glass look-development must reach. We take the exactness, not his forms.); Seaside rock (British lettered rock) and Kintaro-ame (Japanese face-in-every-slice candy) (Things where a name or face runs through the whole length. We deliberately invert their sameness: the order holds, but proportion, colour, twist and temperature visibly change.); Thomas Heatherwick, Extrusions (2009) (Extrusion shown as a living, imperfect sculptural process. Lineage for making the process itself the object.); Experimental Jetset, Whitney Museum identity, the 'Responsive W' (2013) (A mark governed by one rule that adapts to any format. **Self-critique.** Nearest precedents and harsh problems. (1) 'Like a stick of rock, our values run all the way through' is a tired British corporate idiom, and Kintaro-ame is literally a Japanese idiom for monotonous sameness, which is the opposite of 'living'. The core metaphor starts from a cliche and has to earn its way out through the variables. (2) Polymer-clay cane slicing is a huge 'oddly satisfying' video genre. The hero could read as ASMR content rather than a studio thesis. (3) Glass in WebGL is the single most common way to look like stock 3D: refraction, glow, dark backdrop and an orange incandescent rim add up to a generic showreel and the dark-luxury look. Look-development risk is high. (4) Nested rings plus a dot invite bullseye, eye, vinyl record, planet, cable and lollipop readings. The 'lean' must be designed very hard, and client canes risk all looking like sweets. (5) The hex bundles (7, 19) look like honeycombs, molecules or pixel clusters, which edges toward the crowded 'Pixel' visual space this brief warns against. (6) The format press is a superellipse morph that many responsive-logo systems already do, and the Whitney W got there first conceptually. Purists will say the mark is being squashed. (7) Pull is far less discoverable than push and needs an affordance. Horizontal drag on mobile collides with carousels and edge back-swipes. (8) The favicon payoff is invisible to many visitors: Safari's dynamic favicon support is limited, and people do not look at tabs. (9) The AI-first argument is carried mostly by the recipe card and copy. The hero toy shows scaling and multiplication, not learning. (10) A decade of 'slices' risks monotony unless press, colour, time and bundle variables are pushed hard. Murano and vermilion can also tip into souvenir-shop.

**Feasibility.** Stack, consistent with the archived brief: Next.js App Router, one persistent R3F canvas (three.js), GSAP for authored scroll ranges, and SecondOrder springs for input, with per-frame state in mutable stores. THE CANE: a TubeGeometry rebuilt along a CatmullRom spline from a 48-node Verlet chain (fixed timestep, CPU), with a radius profile r(s) driven by the pull state. The interior is analytic: in the local section plane, the refracted view ray is intersected with the eccentric layer circles and the filament (2D ray-circle tests), with Beer-Lambert absorption for the clear casing and opaque hits for the layers, which needs no screen-space refraction. Cut faces use the same 2D SDF. The press is an Lp-norm metric with aspect scaling. Heat is a per-vertex temperature attribute that decays on the CPU and feeds a blackbody ramp. Lighting is one small PMREM environment plus one analytic key, with half-resolution selective bloom on emissive only (desktop). Slices are an InstancedMesh using the same SDF shader. Budget: about 16k vertices and 3–5 draw calls; 60 fps on a 2021 laptop is realistic. MOBILE: 32x128 tube segments, layered alpha instead of interior ray tests, no bloom (emissive tone-mapped), DPR capped at 1.5, and the pull bound to a horizontal stage-only gesture. DOM-FIRST: one JSON recipe feeds both GLSL and a TypeScript SVG generator, with unit tests that topology is preserved at every press and size tier. Without WebGL, sections are SVG, the pull is an accessible range input styled as the neck, cuts append SVG slices, and THE PULL becomes a sticky SVG sequence. The favicon uses canvas and link[rel=icon] in Chromium and Firefox, with a DOM tab mock elsewhere. RISKS: glass look-development (budget a dedicated sprint), aliasing on very thin pulls (switch to flat SDF shading below about 6 px radius), and spring-chain stability at high scroll velocity (clamp it).

**How AI-first shows.** 'We don't redraw; we re-pull.' The identity is a recipe that renders any size, format and medium on demand: systems over one-offs. Every slice is a version, dated by its filament angle, so versioning is visible in the mark itself. Testing means two slices from the same cane side by side, visibly siblings. Learning means the slices that worked are bundled back into the next cane, so v2 physically contains v1. Speed is the pull itself: instant scale, no redrawing. On the site this appears as behaviour: the hero tray as a test bench, the recipe card's version history, and the visitor's slices folded into the final bundle. It never appears as imagery. There are no brains, sparkles or 'generated' aesthetics, and no claims of automated results.

## C — FAMILY MOULD: One shot, every part

> The identity is a precision family mould, engineered once and shot many times. Every shot fills every channel's format in the same instant, from one source, in one material, and every part is marked with where and when it was made.

**Core idea.** Injection moulding with a family tool. A family mould holds cavities for different parts that fill in the same shot, and its runners are balanced so every cavity fills at the same moment. When the tool opens, the parts come out joined on one frame. They are cut free at their gates and carry the marks of their making: gate scar, parting line, ejector witness marks and a date wheel. The leftover runners are ground up and fed into the next shot. The TOOL is the Living Identity: cavities, gate positions and marks never change, while material, colour, finish, fill and inserts change every shot. The SHOT is the Distribution Machine: one source and balanced channels give simultaneous arrival, and the parts are cut free and sent. REGRIND is the learning loop: what comes back feeds the next shot. The website itself is a mould. Its hairline parting line splits the page at every major transition.

**Genome: what never changes**
- The gate: every output has one gate at the centre of its top edge. While in production it is attached as a short tapered neck to a round sprue button; once shipped it is a flat cut scar. The asset's state is visible in its form.
- The parting line: a single hairline inset around every part. It is also the only line that ever splits a page.
- One shot, one material: formats are released as a family that shares one material. Nothing ships alone.
- The date wheel: a small dial in the bottom-right of every part, whose arrow marks shot and version.
- Moulded, not printed: graphics stand in relief with a 2-degree draft edge, and type is a cavity that fills.

**Genome: what adapts**
- Cavity set: which formats are in the family (9:16 story, 4:5 and 1:1 posts, 16:9 film, 1.91:1 link card, billboard band, email header, search snippet, favicon, product tile), re-packed per campaign and per screen.
- Masterbatch: colour per client, campaign, audience or locale.
- Polymer character per medium: clear, translucent, opaque, or glass-filled matte.
- Surface finish per channel: mirror polish through to spark-eroded matte.
- Fill level: short shots for teasers, drafts and tests; full shots for launch.
- Regrind percentage: flecks of earlier shots carried into new parts, as campaign memory.
- Inserts: copy, image inlays, locale and legal lines, swapped without touching the tool.
- Shot number and date wheel, kept in Australian Eastern time, because the day's first shot fires here.

**The signal unit.** THE PART (with its GATE). This describes behaviour, not a final logo. It is a plate of any proportion, joined at the centre of its top edge by a short tapered neck to a round sprue button. The button and neck are fixed relative to the plate's short side; the plate takes any aspect. TRANSFORMS: material, colour, finish and fill change per shot while the geometry holds. MULTIPLIES: one shot yields a whole family of parts on one frame. ADAPTS: the plate's proportion is the channel's format. At 16 px it reduces to button, neck and square, a silhouette that says 'this came from somewhere'. TRAVELS: parts are snipped from the frame with a tick and fly to their channels, keeping the scar as proof of origin. RECONNECTS: the leftover runner frame is reground into the next shot, so every new family contains flecks of earlier ones. At the footer the parts return to their cavities and the tool closes for the next cycle. The wordmark is nine letter-parts filled on one balanced runner. In use it is set as type, with a single gate scar above the final S, the plural, the part that is always being sent.

**Art direction.** Look and feel: a tool room at seven in the morning. Precise, quiet and industrial, never hobby-nostalgic. Camera grammar: top-down three-quarter product photography, macro on witness marks, and tool sheets presented as calm typologies. Composition: the page is a mould. A full-width hairline parting line sits at about 58% of the hero viewport. The headline and statement live on the upper (A) half. The lower (B) half is the steel plate, engraved with a family of format cavities around a central sprue. There is no card and no radius; the parting line is the stage frame. Light: one large overhead softbox raking from the left at about 20 degrees, laying long linear highlights across mirror steel. Polymer parts glow through thickness-based translucency, with thin areas brighter. Palette directions (not final): (1) TOOL ROOM: polished steel greys, a paper-grey page, natural ivory polymer and carbon black, with colour arriving only with a shot. (2) HOUSE SHOT: one dense, slightly warm red-violet masterbatch (in the aubergine-to-beetroot range) as the studio's own material against steel and ink. (3) REGRIND: a neutral base speckled with fine flecks of every previous shot. It explicitly avoids: toy-box primaries, LEGO, grey plastic-soldier nostalgia, the orange/grey/white engineering-gadget palette, circuit-board green, Moldflow rainbow fill plots, chrome blobs, and electric blue with acid green.

**Typography.** Direction 1, ENGRAVED. Display: Söhne Breit (Klim, licensed) or Anybody at expanded widths (open, variable wdth). Part numbers and cavity labels use single-stroke engraving type derived from the public-domain Hershey vector fonts, rendered as hairline grooves in the steel and as SVG strokes in the DOM. Data and UI: Söhne Mono (licensed) or Fragment Mono (open). Direction 2, EDITORIAL TOOL. Display and body: Tiempos Headline and Text (Klim, licensed) or Newsreader (open, Production Type). A calm editorial serif stops the industrial parts from feeling like a gadget brand. Engraved Hershey-derived labels and a mono handle specs. Type behaviour (both): headlines are cavities that fill. Every letter fills from its own top gate at the same time, a 'balanced headline', never a left-to-right stagger. Teaser lines can sit as short shots (partly filled) while the DOM text stays complete for readers and screen readers. Part numbers (PX-0412) and the header shot counter increment live. Editorial composition: tool sheets are large technical posters with generous margins and specs in mono, and content sections stay quiet and single-column.

**3D forms and materials.** THE TOOL: the moving (B) half of a family mould in mirror-polished tool steel. It has machined cavities with draft walls and 0.5 mm inside radii, half-round runners laid as a naturally balanced H-pattern with chunky filleted channels (never thin lines with nodes), a central sprue bushing, flush ejector pins, brass cooling plugs, engraved part numbers and a rotating date-wheel insert. The fixed (A) half is a thick, polished clear plate, as used in flow-visualisation tools, so the melt front can be watched. THE PARTS: thick-walled polymer with a crisp parting line, draft bevels, a gate tab or scar, ejector witness circles on the back, a date-wheel relief, thickness-driven translucency and optional regrind flecks. THE FRAME: the runner skeleton that joins the parts, always ground back into the next shot. Why this is not stock: every feature is a manufacturing truth that encodes provenance. The gate says where it connected, the parting line and draft say how it was made, the date wheel says when, and the regrind says what from. No abstract solid ever floats free; every object is a tool, a part or a runner.

**Motion.** Mechanical, cyclical and simultaneous. (1) Every action is a cycle: close, inject, hold, open, eject, about 1.1 s in the hero. Close takes 120 ms with a hard stop and a two-frame micro-shake. Inject takes 450 ms at constant velocity. Hold takes 120 ms and open 180 ms. Eject takes 220 ms with the only springy overshoot on the site (about 3.5 Hz, damping about 0.5). An instant 40 ms press-down response covers the gap before the cycle runs. (2) No stagger: things arrive together. Balanced runners mean every front reaches every cavity in the same frame, and headlines fill all their letters at once. This is a deliberate break from the web's default cascade reveals. (3) Melt fronts are smooth and rounded with a fountain-flow bulge, never a linear wipe. (4) Heavy things are critically damped: the tool tilts on a stiff spring (about 1.6 Hz, damping 1.0). (5) The one permitted sequence is the snip train, where parts are cut free at 70 ms intervals with a tick each, like a counter. Signature transitions: PART (the page splits along the parting line, the upper half rises and the lower half drops, and the next section's frame hangs between them; scroll-mapped and exact in reverse), SNIP (a part leaves its frame with a 30 ms jolt and keeps its scar) and REGRIND (spent runners crumble into flecks that settle into the next section's material). Reduced motion: shots resolve as a single 150 ms cross-fade to filled, with no shake and no flying parts.

**Hero and interaction.** Hero toy: SHOOT. MOVE: the steel plate tilts toward the cursor on a heavy spring, and the raking highlight slides across the mirror polish and engraved cavities. Hovering a cavity engraves its label (format, size, channel). CLICK/TAP: one shot. The clear plate closes, and the melt front leaves the central sprue, races through the balanced runner and reaches every format cavity in the same frame (thunk). The plate lifts and ejector pins pop the family toward you on its frame. CLICK AGAIN: a new shot in a new material (masterbatch, translucency, finish). The previous frame drops into the hopper, and its colour returns as flecks in the new parts. PRESS AND HOLD (optional depth): you control injection, and releasing early gives a short shot, with partial parts and frozen rounded fronts (teasers). H1: within 3 s you see mirror steel and glossy polymer, the plate answers the cursor, and the plain statement sits above the parting line. H2: the toy is the thesis: one act fills every channel's format, from one source, at once. H3: type lives on the upper half, and the parting line is both the stage frame and the transition device (PART). **Self-critique.** Nearest precedents and harsh problems. (1) Model-kit sprues (Tamiya, Airfix, Gunpla) and the long tail of 'sprue' merch, posters and keychains make this a known graphic trope. The toy and hobby reading is the biggest risk for a premium branding studio. (2) The tool sheet is essentially the agency 'all formats on one slide' asset grid with a material skin. (3) The headline flirts with 'create once, publish everywhere' (COPE), a long-standing content-marketing slogan. The copy needs a sharper, ownable line. (4) The metaphor is mass production, so 'identical copies' is the default reading, which is the opposite of a living, AI-era identity. The variables (fill, regrind, inserts, finish) must be pushed hard or it reads as sameness. (5) Celebrating plastic injection moulding in 2026 can read as environmentally tone-deaf. (7) Recognisability depends on small details (gate tab, date wheel) that vanish at thumbnail scale or get cropped by platform UI. (9) Balanced, simultaneous fill is idealised, and mould engineers will notice. (10) A 1.1 s shot cycle is slower than instant feedback, so it needs a press-down micro-response or the hero feels laggy. (11) Polished steel lives or dies on environment-lighting authoring; without it, it reads as grey CG.

**Feasibility.** Stack, consistent with the archived brief: Next.js, one persistent R3F canvas (three.js), GSAP for scroll ranges, SecondOrder springs, and mutable per-frame stores. The layout is built as analytic rect and capsule SDFs with smooth unions, and shaded with a mirror-steel BRDF against one authored PMREM environment. THE FILL: a fill-time field is precomputed per layout (fast marching on a 512x512 grid in a Web Worker, about 10–30 ms on layout change). THE PARTS: an InstancedMesh of draft-bevelled boxes with relief from per-format SDF textures, faked thickness translucency, and flecks from seeded hash noise sampling a uniform array of up to 8 past masterbatch colours. Open, eject and snip are spring-driven transforms. Parts bind to DOM rects for their flights to channel mockups. Budget: under 10 draw calls; 60 fps on desktop is comfortable. MOBILE: the generator re-packs a portrait plate with 6 cavities, uses normal maps instead of relief marching, caps DPR at 1.5, and uses tap to shoot. DOM-FIRST: the tool sheet exists as SVG generated from the same layout JSON. The fill animates through an SVG mask stepped from the baked fill-time contours. The PART transition is CSS transforms on two page halves. Every format mockup is real HTML with selectable text. **How AI-first shows.** 'We build the tool, not the post.' The operating philosophy shows up in how the system behaves. The tool is engineered once and shot endlessly: systems over one-offs. Short shots are rapid, low-fidelity drafts and teasers released to learn. A/B variants are two inserts moulded side by side in one tool. Learning is regrind: what comes back is ground into the next shot, so later parts physically contain earlier ones. Versioning is the shot number and the date wheel on every part. Adaptation is the generator re-packing the tool live for the visitor's own screen. On the site, the header shot counter counts the visitor's real shots, and the Process section scrubs fill levels. There is no AI imagery, no automation spectacle and no invented results.

## D — In Any Key

> The identity is a phrase, not a picture. The studio's words are cast as tuned aluminium bars whose length (the type's set width) sets their pitch. Strike them with the cursor and the brand plays; change channel and the same tune is transposed into that channel's key and tempo, still recognisable because only the ratios are fixed.

**Core idea.** Transposition. A melody stays itself in any key and at any tempo because its identity lives in the ratios between notes, not in the notes themselves. Pixelards defines a brand the same way. The Living Identity is a tune: a long-short-short metre at 2:1:1 and fixed proportions between the three words of a phrase, set in type whose width literally determines pitch. The Distribution Machine is the arrangement, the same tune written out for every instrument: the roadside plays it low and held, a story plays it higher and faster, a search listing speaks it, an email opens on it. The site shows this as a score in which every channel's downbeat lines up. Reconnection is arithmetic: the two short bars together are exactly as long as the long bar, so they can slide together and become the next downbeat.

**Genome: what never changes**
- The metre: long-short-short at 2:1:1. It sets durations in time-based media, column widths, line lengths and bar lengths in static media, and three-group phrasing in copy.
- The intervals: a phrase's three bars always keep 2:1:1, so its contour (low, then two equal higher notes) never changes in any key.
- The stress rule in type: stressed groups are set heavy and wide, unstressed groups light and narrow, at a fixed contrast (stem weight about 3:1).
- The downbeat colour: only the long element ever carries the signal colour. Everything else is ink.
- One house key: every channel's bars are tuned to notes of one house chord, so all channels sound, and visibly glint, in harmony.
- The sign-off: every piece ends on the beat mark (three bars, 2:1:1), and every sound piece ends on the three-note sting, long-short-short.
- One timing grid: cuts, page transitions, reveals and sound all land on downbeats.

**Genome: what adapts**
- Key: the absolute bar length and type width for each format. Wide formats sound low and narrow formats sound high.
- Tempo per channel: the roadside holds (tempo 0, with the metre translated into layout), a 6 s bumper runs ~120 bpm, a story ~96 bpm, long form ~60 bpm, and email moves at reading pace
- The words: any copy that scans as one stressed group plus two lighter groups, adapted to each locale's stress patterns
- Register: from quiet to loud, mapped to size, weight extremes and sound level
- Instrument (medium): type, sound, edit rhythm, light or layout
- Swing: how much human timing is kept from the input, tighter for institutional clients and looser for culture brands
- Stock and light: the dark 'after hours' or daylight 'score paper' variant, with light temperature set by time of day

**The signal unit.** THE BEAT: three bars side by side, one long and two half-length, separated by gaps of 1/8 of the long bar. In the hero they are physical tuned bars; in print they are three rules; in edits, three cuts; in sound, three notes. TRANSFORMS: transposing scales all three together, so the ratio holds while absolute size, pitch and tempo change. MULTIPLIES: phrases chain into sentences and campaigns, each starting on a downbeat. ADAPTS: in time it plays, and in space it becomes proportion (a 2:1:1 grid, a long headline line over two short ones). TRAVELS: it is the CTA underline, the nav's active state, the loading counter, the rhythm of section transitions and the video sting. RECONNECTS: the two short bars slide together into one bar exactly as long as the first (1 + 1 = 2), which becomes the downbeat of the next phrase, page or channel.

**Art direction.** Dark, quiet and exact, closer to a recording room after hours than a print shop. The page is warm graphite throughout. There is no light page with a card, and the stage is not a card: it is a long ~3:1 letterbox bounded only by two hairline rules, with the bars floating inside it on dark felt cords. LIGHT: one long overhead strip (a rect-area light) lays a single specular streak along each bar, and a low warm rim light comes from the right. When a bar is struck, the streak shivers along its length and the engraved letters flash. CAMERA: 28° down with a long lens (FOV 20-24°) and very little perspective, so the engraved type stays readable. KEYFRAME: three satin bars in a dark field, the long one amber-edged, typography cut into metal, and silence. PALETTE DIRECTIONS (not final): (1) 'After hours': warm graphite, raw satin aluminium and a sodium-amber downbeat. (2) 'Score paper' (daylight and print variant): bone paper, graphite ink and a vermilion downbeat. (3) 'Signal': ink black, bone and cadmium yellow. EXPLICITLY AVOIDS: audio-spectrum and equaliser bars, waveforms, VU meters, piano keys, vinyl, headphones, music-app UI, neon, chrome blobs, purple-blue 'AI' gradients, Apple-style unibody product-render language, staff lines in the hero, and any rounded card on an off-white page.

**Typography.** DIRECTION 1, 'Stress grotesk'. A grotesk with width, weight and grade axes, so stress can move along a line without the line reflowing. Open source: Roboto Flex (its GRAD axis changes weight without changing width, ideal for strikes) and Bricolage Grotesque (opsz, wdth, wght) for display warmth. Licensed: GT America (Grilli Type, Compressed to Extended widths) for the bars and display. DIRECTION 2, 'Spoken serif'. Stress is shown as roman versus italic, the way editors mark emphasis: stressed groups in heavy roman, unstressed groups in light italic. Open source: Source Serif 4 (opsz, wght, true italics). Licensed: GT Sectra (Grilli Type) or Domaine Display (Klim), whose sharp cuts engrave well. COMPOSITION: every headline is set as one long line over two short lines, so the rag itself is the metre. Body copy stays calm and single-size; the metre is carried by the layout grid (2:1:1 columns), not by decoration. Each bar also carries its frequency in small tabular figures ('523 Hz'), stamped the way a tuning fork is. HOW THE TYPE LIVES: words strike in rather than fade in, with a 1-2 frame attack, a grade overshoot of +80 and a 400 ms decay to rest. Stress travels with the beat. On transposition the bar widths re-fit and the frequency figures count to their new values.

**3D forms and materials.** WORD-BARS: extruded aluminium with a rectangular section and 0.6 mm eased edges. The surface is bead-blasted satin with anisotropic roughness along the length, so the overhead strip becomes one long streak. Each word is engraved into the top face with mirror-polished floors, so letters flash as the streak passes over them. A bar's length is the word's set width plus 1 em. Its thickness is constant per channel, nudged to land on the house chord, which is how real metallophone bars are tuned. SUPPORTS: two dark wool-felt cords under each bar at its nodal points, 0.224 × length from each end, where a free bar actually stays still. STRIKE: there is no mallet object. The bar flexes in its first free-free bending mode (a vertex shader, with amplitude decaying exponentially: τ ≈ 0.9 s for long bars, 0.5 s for short) while the streak shimmers. SOUND, when enabled, is modal synthesis using the real partial ratios of a free bar (1 : 2.76 : 5.40 : 8.93), with the fundamental computed from bar length by the inverse-square law, so the type's width literally makes the note. WHY NOT STOCK: the geometry is derived from text metrics, the supports sit where physics puts them, and the material is that of real vibraphone bars. Nothing is a glossy abstract shape, and nothing could be downloaded as a stock asset.

**Motion.** On the beat. All motion is quantised to one tempo grid (100 bpm in the hero). The signature transition is the phrase itself: one long move (2 units) followed by two short moves (1 unit each). For example, a section frame slides in 480 ms, its headline strikes in over 240 ms, then its body over 240 ms. ENVELOPES, NOT EASINGS: elements arrive with a fast attack (≤ 40 ms) and leave on a natural exponential decay. Overshoot happens in weight (grade), never in position. Physical bars use SecondOrder springs tuned to ring (f 3.2 Hz, ζ 0.08) and visibly decay. RESTS MATTER: every phrase is followed by one full beat of stillness. Scroll-driven sequences map beats to scroll ranges, so everything stays a pure function of progress and reverses exactly. Character: percussive, crisp and musical, never floaty.

**Hero and interaction.** HERO TOY: PLAY THE NAME. H1 (3 s): moving the pointer across the bars strikes each bar it crosses. The bar flexes, a light streak runs its length and its letters flash, with pointer speed setting the strength. This reads with sound off, which is the default, and the DOM sentence above says plainly what the studio does. H2: a click transposes. The letterbox morphs to the next channel's aspect (roadside ~4:1, then story 9:16, square 1:1, a search line, then email). The bars re-cast to new lengths as the words re-fit, the frequency figures update, and the phrase plays itself once in the new key and tempo: visibly the same tune in a new place, which is the thesis. H3: the sentence has its own block above the stage. The stage's two hairlines later open to full-bleed and become the outer rules of the Score. H4: the preloader's lengthening bar becomes the hero's long bar. H5: every click brings a new channel, a new verse and a new key with the same tune, and strikes can be replayed endlessly. H6: the visitor's strikes are recorded and quantised to the metre while keeping their swing (their human timing offsets). This becomes 'your motif'. It plays as glints (and sound, if on) at each section transition, sets the hover rhythm of the nav, and at Contact is arranged across six channels as a downloadable 6-second loop and a poster whose column widths are the rhythm. CURSOR: the system cursor over content. Over the stage it becomes a slim horizontal dash (the short bar) that stretches with velocity. KEYBOARD: Tab to the stage, keys 1, 2 and 3 strike, Space transposes. A live region announces, for example, 'Story key, 96 beats per minute: Made, to, move.' TOUCH: tap a bar to strike it, and a visible 'Next channel' button transposes. There are no drags on the stage, so scrolling is never captured. REDUCED MOTION: no flex, just a static highlight and caption, and transposition is instant. SOUND: an opt-in toggle at the stage edge, unlocked by the first gesture after opting in. It is mastered quietly (around -18 LUFS) and never autoplays.

**Generative system.** INPUTS: a message, a channel spec (format, duration, context), the house key (a chord), a tempo table, a swing value and a stock. RULES: (1) Scan: split the copy into phrases of one stressed group plus two lighter groups. A language-model step proposes splits and alternative wordings that scan; a rule checker rejects any that don't (group length limits, stress position, a locale stress dictionary). (2) Fit: solve each group's width axis so the bar lengths hit 2:1:1 inside the channel's measure. (3) Key: derive pitch from bar length (inverse-square), then snap it to the house chord via bar thickness. (4) Time: in time-based media, durations are 2:1:1 beats at the channel tempo, and cuts land on downbeats. (5) Space: in static media the metre becomes layout (2:1:1 columns, a long line over two short ones, heavy-light-light). (6) Sign off with the beat mark and the sting. OUTPUTS: a 6 s bumper, a 15 s story (three phrases), a 30 s spot, static posts, OOH (one phrase, low key, held), an email subject and preheader (the long group as subject, the two short groups as preheader), a search title and description, a product page on a 2:1:1 grid, UI motion timings derived from the tempo table, an audio sting and on-hold audio, and even merch: a desk set of three tuned bars engraved with a client's phrase. Each client receives their own tune, house key and tempo table, so a decade of outputs comes from the same few ratios.

**Distribution, made visible.** 'THE SCORE', the immersive set piece. A full-bleed, typeset orchestral score is pinned for at most ~2.5 viewport heights while scroll advances a playhead across it (scrubbed, reversible). Seven rows are labelled at the left in small caps: Roadside, Story, Post, Email, Search, Product page, Spot. Every row carries the same phrase as tuned bars, at that channel's key (bar length) and tempo (spacing). Vertical bar lines run through all the rows, so downbeats visibly align across every channel. As the playhead crosses a row, its live miniature plays its own version. The 9:16 frame cuts on the beat. The search listing types out its title and description with the stressed group in place. **Signature moment.** THE CHORD. Satin aluminium under a strip light flirts with Apple product-render language, and its dark chiaroscuro with anisotropic streaks echoes the archived Warp & Weft. The biggest weakness is that the idea's best payoff (hearing the same tune higher on a story) needs sound, and sound must be off by default. Silent, the hero risks being 'shiny bars that wobble', and the width-to-pitch insight is invisible to most visitors. Forcing the 2:1:1 metre onto all copy risks three-word slogan soup ('Found when asked', 'Made to move'). 'Pixelards' only scans as long-short-short if nobody stresses '-ards'. SEO, AI search and automation map onto music only by analogy, and 'words that scan' is thin for AI search. Engraved type on bars seen at an angle is less legible than flat type, so the typographic lens is partly served through objects rather than letterforms. The hero's structure (pointer gives a physical response, click gives a variation) is structurally close to the reference's toy, even though the object and meaning differ. The trace, a remembered rhythm, is subtler than a visible artefact, so many visitors won't notice the payoff. Build risk is real: modal synthesis, mobile audio unlock, variable-font rasterisation for engraving and the shader vibration all have to be excellent, or this is a novelty.

**Feasibility.** BARS: procedural box geometry per word, with length from text metrics computed at the solved axis values (fontkit, which supports variable fonts, or harfbuzzjs in a worker). ENGRAVING: each word's glyph outlines, taken from fontkit at those axis values, are rendered to a 1K signed-distance texture per bar and regenerated only on transposition. The SDF drives the polished-floor mask, normals and roughness, so no geometry booleans are needed, and the canvas-font limitation with variable axes is avoided. VIBRATION: a vertex shader with first-mode shape and decay uniforms, instanced: ≤ 7 bars on stage and ≤ 21 in the Score. LIGHTING: three.js RectAreaLight (LTC) on desktop, and a baked environment strip on mobile and low tiers. AUDIO: WebAudio modal synthesis (4 partials per strike, negligible CPU) plus one recorded sting sample. It unlocks on the first gesture after opt-in, with careful iOS handling. SCORE: mostly DOM/SVG (labels, bar lines, live miniatures as DOM, with short pre-rendered MP4 loops where needed), with WebGL bars bound to DOM rects. It is pinned via ScrollTrigger as a pure function of progress, with native scroll preserved and the pin length capped. FALLBACK: without WebGL, the bars are DOM elements with CSS gradients, strikes are CSS transform animations, text is real DOM and sound still works, so the thesis survives. BUDGETS: ≤ 30 draw calls; 60 fps on a 2021 laptop; ≥ 30 fps on mid-range mobile with LTC off; fonts subset WOFF2 with swap and metric-matched fallbacks; 3D lazy-loaded after text is interactive. EXPORT of the visitor's motif loop uses canvas captureStream plus MediaRecorder (WebM, or MP4 where supported). RISKS: legibility of engraved type at an oblique angle (keep the camera at ≤ 28° and the engraving deep); the pinned Score feeling like scroll-jacking; and audio latency on low-end Android.

**How AI-first shows.** The system behaves like a session player. Give it any brief and any channel, and the phrase comes back instantly in the right key and tempo, kept in tune by rules rather than by someone redrawing it. The site makes four behaviours visible, with no AI imagery at all. QUANTISE: the visitor's ragged strikes snap to the metre but keep their swing: human feel inside machine precision. TRANSPOSE: one click re-arranges the identity for a new channel. TAKES: every arrangement is a numbered take, and alternatives sit side by side; tests are framed as takes, never as invented outcomes. THE SCORE: one rule generating a family of outputs, visibly aligned. In the studio, language models draft wordings that scan, a rule checker validates them, and people choose. The learning loop shows up as a take being promoted to master after a real test, recorded in a visible changelog of the studio's own phrases rather than as a chart.

## E — EXPOSURE: One gesture, every shutter speed

> The identity is a single authored movement of light. Every channel is a different exposure of that same movement: a dot at a thousandth of a second, an arc in a story, the full signature on a billboard.

**Core idea.** A brand is a gesture, not a picture. The Pixelards signal is one designed movement: a single point of light travelling a fixed path at a fixed speed profile, about 2.4 s long.
- **Channels are shutter speeds.** Every channel is a *photograph of that movement at a different exposure*, which maps directly onto how much attention the channel gets:

  | Exposure | Channel | What it shows |
  |---|---|---|
  | 1/1000 s | favicon, avatar | a dot |
  | 1/8 s | story, notification | a short arc |
  | 1 s | feed post | half the path |
  | full 2.4 s | billboard, print | the complete trace |
  | strobe (×5) | email header | the point at five instants |
  | live, real time | 6 s bumper | the point moving |

- **Living Identity:** the gesture, which evolves slowly over the years and is versioned.
- **Distribution Machine:** the camera that re-exposes it for every context.
- **Reconnects:** any two exposures of the same gesture line up perfectly when overlaid, because they are slices of one movement.

**Genome: what never changes**
- One light, one path. The authored signature curve never changes within a version.
- One speed profile, which makes the trace brightness profile constant. Slow parts of the path burn brighter (physically correct light painting), so even a static print shows the *rhythm* of the movement.
- The trace always starts at the same anchor point (the "strike"), which sits on the layout's top-left anchor.
- **Halation rule:** the light always carries a thin warm halo, as film emulsion does, never a neon glow.
- Exposure data is always printed with the image in mono (e.g. `1/8 s · 0.3–0.42 of path · v3`).

**Genome: what adapts**
- Exposure duration and window (which slice of the path), per channel.
- Strobe count for print and email.
- The ground the light passes over or through: black, real client photography, or a 3D space it lights.
- Light colour temperature: tungsten, daylight or sodium.
- Framing and aspect.
- The path version, one per year: a deliberate evolution of the gesture.

**The signal unit.** The trace.
- **Transforms:** exposure changes its length.
- **Multiplies:** one movement yields infinitely many exposures.
- **Adapts:** shutter speed is set by the channel's attention span.
- **Travels:** the live point *is* the site's cursor, loading indicator and section transition.
- **Reconnects:** overlaying exposures re-forms the full gesture.

**Art direction.** Photographic, nocturnal and exact: a darkroom, not a nightclub.
- **Ground:** deep warm black with real photographic grain.
- **Light:** a single point source with true long-exposure physics: additive accumulation, brightness inversely proportional to speed, and a red-orange halation fringe.
- **A daylight counter-mode** for print: an overexposed paper-white ground where the trace is the *shadow* of the light (a negative).
- **Palette directions (not final):**
  1. *Halide*: soot black, tungsten white, halation red.
  2. *Negative*: paper white, graphite trace, cyan halo.
  3. *Sodium*: night-blue black, sodium amber.
- **Avoids:** neon, synthwave, lens flares, bokeh dust, sparkles, light-saber trails, and any "glow" that is not physically accumulated.

**Typography.**
- **Direction 1:** a sober grotesk with optical sizes (open: Inter Display, or Geist; licensed: Söhne) for statements, plus a camera-data mono for exposure data (open: JetBrains Mono; licensed: Berkeley Mono).
- **Direction 2:** a high-contrast didone-like display (open: Bodoni Moda; licensed: GT Super). Its thin hairlines echo light traces.
- **How type lives:** headlines are *exposed in*. They appear as if light is drawn across them left to right, then fix.

**3D forms and materials.**
- The light is a real emissive point in a simple 3D space. Its trail is an accumulation render target (feedback buffer), with physically based falloff and halation in post.
- The trace can **pass through** custom forms:
  - a single sculptural studio object (a matte plaster "plinth-wall" architecture) that the light reveals as it moves, light painting a space;
  - the trace casting moving shadows of type set as thin metal cut-outs.
- **Why not stock:** there are no objects for their own sake. Every form exists to be revealed by the gesture.

**Motion.**
- **Rhythm:** the 2.4 s gesture is the master clock, and all transitions are fractions of it.
- **Transitions are exposures:**
  - leaving a section closes the shutter (the frame freezes as a still with its exposure data);
  - entering opens it.

**Hero and interaction.**
- **Hold = shutter.** Press and hold anywhere on the stage: the light runs the signature path for as long as you hold, and release fixes the exposure as a photograph. A short tap gives a dot or arc (story); a long hold gives the full signature (billboard).
- **Repeatable reward:** every exposure is different and lands in a contact strip under the headline, labelled with its format and shutter speed.
- **Trace:** your exposures persist (session). They become the section markers and return at Contact as your contact sheet.
- **Cursor:** the cursor itself is a live point leaving a 1/15 s trace.
- **3-second test:** the idle light keeps drawing the signature by itself, so craft is visible at once. The first press answers instantly. The sentence sits beside the stage.
- **Keyboard:** Space holds the shutter.
- **Touch:** press and hold. No drags, so scroll is never captured.

**Generative system.**
- **Inputs:** channel spec (aspect, size, attention seconds), ground and colour temperature.
- **Outputs:** exposure window, strobe count and framing, rendered deterministically from the same gesture. A single render pass produces stills, loops and print.
- **Versioning:** yearly gesture versions are archived and shown side by side as the identity's evolution.

**Distribution, made visible.** *"One second, every channel."*
- **Set piece:** a scroll-scrubbed timeline of one 2.4 s gesture, shown simultaneously as eight exposures in true format: favicon dot, notification, story arc, feed post, email strobe, 6 s bumper (live point), billboard (full trace) and a search listing whose title carries the strike mark.
- **Scroll is time.** As you scrub, every format updates in sync.
- **Captions** state shutter speed and format only. No metrics.

**Signature moment.** *The overlay.* All eight exposures slide on top of each other and fuse, without any adjustment, into the one complete gesture.

| 7 Contact | Your contact sheet of exposures | Write |

**References (lineage, not to copy).**
- Gjon Mili's 1949 light-drawing photographs of Picasso (gesture as image).
- Étienne-Jules Marey's chronophotography (one movement, many instants).
- Harold Edgerton's stroboscopic photography.
- Hiroshi Sugimoto's *Theaters* (a whole film in one exposure).
- CineStill-style halation as a physical (not stylistic) effect.

**Self-critique.**
- Light painting and light trails are a well-worn photography and WebGL genre, so the risk of reading as "neon trails" is high. It must be disciplined as photography, not effects.
- The identity depends on drawing **one excellent path**, which is a logo decision in disguise.
- Static recognisability at small sizes depends on the strike mark.
- The halation-film look is currently fashionable.
- Weakest on the search and AI-search capabilities, which are expressed only by analogy.

**Feasibility.** High.
- The feedback-buffer accumulation is cheap.
- The 3D space is optional.
- Exposures can be pre-rendered for static formats.
- Mobile is fine.
- DOM-first fallback: pre-rendered exposures.

**How AI-first shows.** One authored gesture is rendered deterministically into any number of formats by a system. That is the studio's production philosophy, shown rather than claimed.

## F — SELECTION: Brands that evolve in the wild

> Every output is a variant bred from one genome. Channels are environments, testing is selection, and the fittest variants seed the next generation. The visitor does the selecting.

**Core idea.** Living identity, taken literally: **evolution**.
- **The Living Identity is a species:** a fixed skeleton (the genome) expressed as endless individual variants.
- **The Distribution Machine is the field trial:** variants are released into channel environments. Each environment *selects for different traits*: a 16 px favicon selects for simplicity, a billboard for contrast at distance, a story for motion, a search listing for words.
- **Adaptive radiation:** over generations, the brand develops channel-adapted forms that are still unmistakably one species.
- **AI-first, made honest:** this is how AI-assisted creative production with performance testing actually works (generate variants, test, select, iterate). Humans supply taste and the rules of the genome.

**Genome: what never changes**
- **The skeleton:** a fixed topology of three nodes and two strokes, with a characteristic silhouette. It never changes, so the species is always recognisable.
- One "species colour" that every variant carries somewhere.
- Every variant carries a specimen label: `PX · gen.04 · no.117 · parent no.083`.
- Symmetry rule: never mirrored.

**Genome: what adapts**
- Proportion, stroke weight, terminal shape, surface (matte, gloss, cast, printed), ornament density, colour within a range, motion signature, and channel-specific simplification.

**The signal unit.** The specimen.
- **Transforms:** mutation.
- **Multiplies:** each generation produces eight offspring.
- **Adapts:** the channel environment selects.
- **Travels:** specimens are "released" into formats.
- **Reconnects:** lineages converge. The family tree visibly joins back to the root.

**Art direction.** A natural-history museum crossed with a contemporary design studio.
- **The look:** specimen drawers, typological grids (Becher-like rigour), crisp object photography of 3D-printed or ceramic specimens on pins, and labels.
- **Palette directions (not final):**
  1. *Cabinet*: museum card stock, ink, one species colour (signal orange).
  2. *Field*: eucalyptus-grey and bone, with a single coral species colour.
  3. *Night collection*: black velvet drawer, bone specimens.
- **Avoids:** Victorian curiosity-shop kitsch, DNA helices, neural nets, sci-fi "lab" blue, and "AI evolution" glowing cells.

**Typography.**
- **Direction 1:** a scientific serif with optical sizes for Latin-name-style labels (open: Newsreader; licensed: Tiempos), plus a typewriter-style mono for specimen data (open: Courier Prime; licensed: Söhne Mono).
- **Direction 2:** a rational grotesk (open: Inter; licensed: Neue Haas Unica) for a colder, lab-catalogue tone.
- **How type lives:** labels re-number live, and headlines are set as taxonomies (genus, species, variant).

**3D forms and materials.**
- **Specimens:** each is a small, precise 3D object, an extruded and bevelled form of the skeleton. Materials vary per variant: matte ceramic, cast resin, anodised metal, printed nylon.
- **Mounting:** specimens sit pinned in a shallow drawer, with soft overhead museum light and contact shadows.
- **Why not stock:** every object is a deterministic expression of the genome. Nothing is decorative.

**Motion.**
- **Mutation is visible:** offspring morph out of the parent, with a short "budding" animation (~600 ms).
- **Selection:** the chosen specimen lifts, and the others settle and fade into the archive.
- **Transitions** pull out of the drawer (a z-move) into the next section.

**Hero and interaction.**
- **The drawer:** nine specimens of the Pixelards mark. **Click one to select it as a parent**, and the drawer breeds eight new offspring around it. Mutations are visible and the family resemblance is obvious.
- **Repeatable reward:** every click produces a new generation.
- **Trace:** your lineage (the chain of your selections) is recorded as a family tree. It returns at Contact, where your final variant becomes your enquiry card.
- **3-second test:** craft is visible in the specimen materials. Hovering a specimen turns it and reveals its label. The statement sits beside the drawer.
- **Keyboard:** arrow keys move between specimens; Enter selects.
- **Touch:** tap.

**Generative system.**
- **Inputs:** genome ranges and channel constraints (minimum size, contrast, duration, safe areas).
- **Outputs:** valid variants only; the constraints reject unfit ones. Each output is a seeded, reproducible specimen with an ID, so any variant can be regenerated exactly.

**Distribution, made visible.** *"Field trial."*
- **Release:** your selected variant is released into seven channel environments: favicon, story, post, OOH, email, search and product tile.
- **Pressure:** each environment applies its selection pressure, shown as plain rules (e.g. "16 px: details below 2 px are lost").
- **Divergence:** the variant visibly diverges into seven channel-adapted forms, Darwin's-finches style, and the family tree shows them all branching from the root.
- **No fake performance numbers:** fitness is the channel's spec, not invented CTRs.

**Signature moment.** *Adaptive radiation.* One specimen splits into seven subspecies at once, each perfectly fitted to its channel, and the tree draws back to the single root.

| 1 Hero | The drawer of nine specimens beside the statement | Click to breed |

| 3 Field trial | Release into seven channels; adaptive radiation | Scrub |
| 4 Work | A calm specimen-catalogue index of projects | Open |
| 5 Capabilities | Each capability as a selection pressure | Hover |
| 6 Studio | The studio's own lineage across versions | — |

**References (lineage, not to copy).**
- Ernst Haeckel, *Kunstformen der Natur* (1904): typological plates.
- Bernd and Hilla Becher: typologies.
- Karl Sims, *Evolved Virtual Creatures* (1994).
- William Latham's evolutionary art (1990s).
- Darwin's finches as the textbook case of adaptive radiation.

**Self-critique.**
- "Evolution" and "Darwin" are well-trodden agency metaphors.
- Evolutionary-art precedents (Sims, Latham) are famous.
- The specimen/cabinet aesthetic is fashionable and can become kitsch.
- Unconstrained mutations produce ugly marks, so the genome ranges need real design work.
- "Breeding" brand marks may feel cold or gimmicky to clients who want their brand protected, not mutated.
- The base skeleton is effectively a logo decision.

**Feasibility.** High.
- Specimens are procedural extrusions (cheap), with a few materials and a drawer scene.
- No heavy simulation.
- DOM-first fallback: a static specimen grid.

**How AI-first shows.** Most directly of all six: generate, test, select, iterate is shown as the creative method, with humans setting the genome and making the choices.
